## LinkedIn — launch post

We have started formalising a part of MENA economic research that is usually treated as invisible plumbing: the evidence trail itself.

Research Release 001 from the MENA Open Data & Evidence Lab brings seven programmes under one provenance architecture: inflation and price measurement, labour-market comparability, firm AI adoption and labour adjustment, statistical revisions and vintages, procurement intelligence, the economic release calendar, and evidence lineage.

The first new forensic result is small but revealing. In four matched Jordan Department of Statistics public-file pairs that we audited, the English file carried a later archive timestamp than the Arabic file by 3, 6, 13 and 16 calendar days.

That is not a claim that the statistic was unavailable in English everywhere during those gaps. We are measuring the public archive record, not inventing a first-dissemination time. The distinction matters. If a researcher is reconstructing what was visible on a particular date, language, file identity and vintage can become part of the data.

The release includes the underlying audit table, methodology limits, programme registry and the evidence state of the Lab's current research objects.

## LinkedIn — research-forensics post

A statistical value can stay exactly the same while its evidence trail changes.

We matched four Arabic/English file pairs in Jordan's public statistical archive. The English-file timestamps followed the Arabic-file timestamps by 3 to 16 calendar days in the observed pairs.

The interesting question is not “which language was first?” The interesting question is what a historical researcher should record when the archive itself has language-specific timing.

Our answer is to preserve both files, both timestamps, the language, retrieval date and vintage, and to refuse to silently relabel an archive timestamp as the first official dissemination time.

This is the first item in the Lab's Release Forensics series.

## Follow-up — labour comparability

“Unemployment rate” is not a complete variable name.

Our first labour-market comparability note traces Jordan, Egypt and Morocco and records the age framework, population perimeter, denominator and classification architecture that sit behind the headline rate.

All three centre published unemployment measurement on a 15+ framework, but the populations and classification systems attached to that headline differ materially. Those fields need to travel with the number if a regional comparison is supposed to mean anything.
