# Research Release 005 — launch pack

## Primary public post

I have been looking at a fairly simple question: when a statistical office changes the way GDP is measured, what else changes with it?

The answer is more interesting than the revision itself.

I reconstructed four MENA cases.

In Saudi Arabia, the official description of 2023 moves from a 0.8% contraction to 0.5% growth after the comprehensive revision.

In Tunisia, a 6% increase in the measured 2015 GDP level pushes the 2020 public-debt ratio from 88.6% to 83.5% of GDP, even though that does not mean the debt stock suddenly fell by 5.1% of GDP.

In Morocco, 2014 GDP rises 8.2%, but historical growth is not simply revised upward: 2016 falls from 1.1% to 0.5% while 2017 rises from 4.2% to 5.1%. The measured tertiary share also moves from 51.6% to 54.8%.

Jordan is the strangest downstream case. The World Bank says the country's comprehensive national-accounts revision found the economy to be nearly 10% larger than previously estimated. Together with 2025 growth, that was enough to push Jordan from lower-middle to upper-middle income in the FY27 classification.

So I do not think "how large was the GDP revision?" is the most useful question.

I am trying to measure the propagation chain instead:

measurement system → revised statistic → derived ratio / threshold → economic narrative or institutional consequence.

The dataset, source manifest and arithmetic are public. The four cases are deliberately not treated as a representative MENA sample, and I am not turning them into a fake one-number country ranking.

Next step is to freeze a larger episode universe and test how often each propagation mechanism occurs.

## Short post

A GDP revision can do four very different things.

Saudi Arabia: turn a reported contraction into growth.

Tunisia: lower a debt/GDP ratio because GDP, the denominator, was revised upward.

Morocco: rewrite the measured structure of the economy while revising different growth years in opposite directions.

Jordan: help push a country across a World Bank income-classification threshold.

I reconstructed all four from official-source evidence for MENA Evidence Lab.

The object I care about now is not revision size. It is **revision propagation**.

## Do not post these claims

- "MENA GDP is understated by X%."
- "Jordan became 10% richer because of rebasing."
- "Tunisia's debt fell by 5.1 percentage points because of a statistical revision."
- "Saudi Arabia was actually growing in 2023."
- "Large statistical revisions mean bad statistics."

Each overstates what the source evidence establishes.
