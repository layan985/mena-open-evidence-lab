When Economic History Changes Sign

Narrative reversals in revised MENA national accounts

Layan Oraidi · MENA Open Data & Evidence Lab

Abstract

Official economic statistics are revised routinely, but not every revision changes the substantive conclusion an analyst would draw. This note defines a narrative reversal as a comparable later official vintage that crosses a qualitative boundary implied by an earlier vintage: sign, ranking, threshold, or direction. A deliberately selected primary-source pilot contains eight comparable annual real-GDP-growth pairs from Saudi Arabia, Morocco and Tunisia. One pair changes sign: Saudi Arabia’s 2023 real GDP growth moves from −0.8% in an earlier official vintage to +0.5% after the comprehensive national-accounts revision, changing the description of the year from contraction to growth. The remaining seven comparisons change magnitude without changing sign. The pilot is not a prevalence estimate. Its purpose is to establish an auditable classification rule, publish negative evidence alongside positive cases, and define the data architecture required for a larger real-time revision panel.

1. The object

Economic data are usually stored as if each observation has one value. In real time, the relevant object has at least two time dimensions: the period being measured and the vintage at which the measurement was public. Let y(t,v) denote the official value for reference period t as published in vintage v.

For a qualitative classification rule Q(·), a narrative reversal occurs when:

NR(t; v₁,v₂) = 1{ Q(y(t,v₁)) ≠ Q(y(t,v₂)) }

For the simplest sign case, Q(y) distinguishes negative from positive growth. A revision may therefore be economically large without being a narrative reversal, and a smaller revision may be narratively important if the earlier value sits close to a boundary.

2. Admission rule

A case enters the Narrative Reversal Registry only if four conditions hold:

  1. Same economic object. Geography, indicator concept and reference period must match, or a documented bridge must justify comparison.
  2. Comparable official vintages. Both values must be traceable to the statistical authority or another primary official release.
  3. Qualitative boundary crossed. The revision changes sign, ranking, threshold status, or acceleration/deceleration direction.
  4. Measurement claim only. A revised historical value is a change in statistical measurement, not a claim that underlying historical activity changed retroactively.

Morocco and Tunisia therefore supply useful negative evidence: national-accounts rebasing can materially change measured growth without changing the qualitative growth/contraction classification.

4. Saudi Arabia 2023

The earlier official Saudi annual estimate records real GDP growth in 2023 at −0.8%. GASTAT’s comprehensive revision subsequently records +0.5%. The +1.3-point revision crosses zero. This is the pilot’s confirmed narrative reversal and is published separately as Measurement Anomaly 001 with its source chain and machine-readable vintage pair.

5. Why the negative cases matter

A registry that publishes only dramatic flips would be structurally biased toward surprising examples. The research design therefore records revision systems that have been tested and do not cross the relevant boundary. In the current pilot, Morocco’s base-year change materially revises several annual growth rates while preserving positive growth in the observed rows. Tunisia’s rebasing likewise changes measured growth in the documented examples while preserving the sign of growth or contraction.

This distinction separates revision magnitude from narrative instability. It also creates the denominator needed for future estimates of reversal frequency once complete within-country vintage panels are assembled.

6. From reversal to fragility

The companion Narrative Fragility Experiment asks whether a first-release estimate’s vulnerability to later reversal can be measured before the revision arrives. For threshold c and future revision R, the object is:

Fragility(y,c) = P[(y−c)(y+R−c) < 0]

Under a zero-mean Normal revision process, an estimate 0.5 revision standard deviations from a threshold has a 30.9% crossing probability; at one standard deviation the probability is 15.9%, at two it is 2.28%, and at three it is 0.135%. A seeded equal-variance Student-t(3) simulation shows that heavy tails produce fewer crossings close to the threshold but more crossings in the far tail, with the relationship reversing at roughly 2.1 standard deviations in the experiment.

7. What this pilot cannot establish

The 12.5% sign-reversal share in this eight-observation pilot is not a MENA reversal rate. The observations were selected because primary pre/post revision values could be verified. Countries, years and revision events are not sampled representatively. No inferential claim is made from the pilot proportion.

The full Saudi 2011–2023 pre/post series is still awaiting archival retrieval of the two original GASTAT workbooks. Jordan’s comprehensive revision panel is also pending. Empirical country-level Narrative Fragility probabilities will not be published until revision distributions are estimated from archived comparable vintages and evaluated out of sample.

8. Research programme

The next release has three gates. First, archive complete within-country vintage histories, beginning with Saudi Arabia. Second, estimate the empirical distribution of revisions and the frequency of sign, threshold, ranking and direction reversals. Third, test whether standardized distance to a qualitative boundary predicts realized reversals out of sample. The intended end product is not a collection of revision anecdotes, but a real-time measurement-risk layer for official economic statistics.
