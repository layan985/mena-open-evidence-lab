How fragile is a first estimate before the revision arrives?

A first-release number can be statistically close to a qualitative boundary even when it looks precise. This experiment defines narrative fragility as the probability that a future revision crosses that boundary.

Fragility(y,c) = P[(y − c)(y + R − c) < 0]

Here y is the first-release estimate, c is the qualitative threshold and R is the later revision. For GDP growth with a growth/contraction boundary, c = 0.

Normal benchmark

If revisions are centered on zero and the first estimate sits d revision-standard-deviations from the threshold, the one-sided flip probability is Φ(−d).

Simulation result

Heavy tails can reduce fragility near the threshold — then dominate it far away.

Using one million seeded draws per distribution, a unit-variance Student-t(3) revision process produces fewer threshold crossings than a Normal process when the first estimate is close to the boundary. The t distribution is more peaked around zero. But beyond roughly 2.10 revision standard deviations, the fat tails dominate.

At 2.5 standard deviations, the simulated t(3) flip rate is about 1.83× the Normal benchmark. At 3 standard deviations, it is about 5.13×.

This means “heavy-tailed revisions are more dangerous” is incomplete. The answer depends on how far the first estimate sits from the decision boundary.

Why this matters empirically

The Saudi 2023 case motivates the question but does not calibrate the model.

Observed case

Saudi Arabia 2023

Official real GDP growth changed from −0.8% to +0.5% after the comprehensive revision. That is a realized narrative reversal.
